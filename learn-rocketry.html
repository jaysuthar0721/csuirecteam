New to Rocketry?

A plain-English guide to getting started in rocketry.

You don't need an engineering degree to get into this hobby! Here's what the jargon means, how people actually get started, and where to go for more.

Getting Started

How do I get into rocketry?

Step 1

Fly model rockets

Start small. Low-power model rockets (motor classes A through C) don't require any certification and are widely sold at hobby shops. They're the easiest way to learn the basics: stability, motor selection, and safe recovery.

Step 2

Get high-power certified

Once you're ready for bigger motors, you'll need a High-Power Rocketry (HPR) certification through the National Association of Rocketry (NAR) or Tripoli Rocketry Association. Level 1 opens up H and I motors, Level 2 opens J through L, and Level 3 opens the largest motors available to hobbyists.

Step 3

Join a club

Clubs like Ram Rocketry give you hands-on experience building full vehicles, mentorship from members who've already certified, and access to bigger, more ambitious projects than most people can take on alone.

Step 4

Compete

Put it all together at a competition like IREC, where teams design, build, and fly a complete rocket to a precise target altitude, or NASA's Student Launch program.

Certification Levels

What HPR certification actually unlocks.

Certification isn't a class, it's a real flight you complete under the supervision of a certified member. Each level lets you legally buy and fly bigger motors.

Entry Point

Level 1

Unlocks H and I motors. This is the first real step into high-power rocketry, and the certification Ram Rocketry's in-house L1 kit is built for.

Written Exam Required

Level 2

Unlocks J, K, and L motors. Requires a written exam in addition to a successful flight, since these motors carry a lot more energy.

Advanced

Level 3

Unlocks the largest hobby motors (M and above). Usually involves a larger, more complex vehicle and a longer review process.

Ready to certify? The official process runs through NAR or Tripoli, and Ram Rocketry members can certify with the club.

Glossary

Rocketry terms, in plain English.

Jargon you'll see across our site and everywhere else in the hobby.

Apogee

The highest point of a rocket's flight, right before it starts falling back down.

Dual-Deploy Recovery

A parachute system that releases a small drogue chute at apogee to control the fall, then a larger main chute closer to the ground for a soft landing.

Motor Class (A–O)

Hobby motors are graded by total impulse, from A (smallest) up to O (largest). Each letter roughly doubles the power of the one before it.

High-Power Rocketry (HPR)

Rocketry using H-impulse motors and above. Flying these motors requires certification.

COTS

Short for "Commercial Off-The-Shelf": a component, usually a motor, that's bought pre-made rather than built by the team.

Airframe

The rocket's outer body tube, the structure everything else attaches to.

Avionics

The onboard electronics that track flight data (altitude, acceleration, GPS) and trigger recovery events like parachute deployment.

Airbrake

A mechanism that extends flaps mid-flight to add drag, used to fine-tune a rocket's altitude so it hits a precise target.

Bi-Propellant

A liquid rocket engine that uses two separate chemicals, a fuel and an oxidizer, that combust when mixed together.

Pintle Injector

A type of injector for liquid engines that mixes fuel and oxidizer through a single, adjustable opening.

Static Fire

A ground test where an engine is ignited while bolted in place, so the team can test performance without an actual flight.

Nosecone

The aerodynamic tip of a rocket, shaped to cut through the air and reduce drag.

Helpful Resources

Where to go next.

A few trusted sources outside our site worth bookmarking.

National Association of Rocketry ↗

The official certification process, safety code, and local club finder for NAR, one of the two major U.S. rocketry organizations.

Tripoli Rocketry Association ↗

The other major U.S. rocketry organization, with its own certification process, prefecture (local club) directory, and safety code.

What Is IREC? (ESRA) ↗

The official explainer from the Experimental Sounding Rocket Association, which runs the International Rocket Engineering Competition.

OpenRocket ↗

The free, open-source flight simulation software that Ram Rocketry and most teams in the hobby use to model rocket flights before they happen.

Rocketry Forum ↗

A large, active community forum where hobbyists share builds, ask questions, and troubleshoot problems.

Apogee's Peak of Flight ↗

A long-running newsletter covering rocketry design, technique, and news, from a well-known hobby motor and supplies retailer.

MIT Rocket Team Wiki ↗

A detailed rocketry knowledge base from MIT's collegiate rocket team, covering everything from propellant chemistry to avionics design.

Nakka's Experimental Rocketry ↗

A long-running, deeply technical reference on amateur rocket motor design, propellant chemistry, and testing from rocketry veteran Richard Nakka.
